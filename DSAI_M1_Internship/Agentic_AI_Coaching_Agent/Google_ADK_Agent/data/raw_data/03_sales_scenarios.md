# SPIN Sales Scenarios — Practice Conversations

## Scenario 1: Small Urban Veterinary Clinic

### Context
- Clinic: PawCare Vet, single veterinarian practice in a suburban area
- 800 patients/month, 70% dogs, 30% cats
- Currently using a competitor's topical flea/tick product
- Compliance rate for prevention: ~25% of patients
- Budget-conscious, owner makes all purchasing decisions

### Ideal SPIN Conversation

**Situation (2 min)**
Rep: "Dr. Martinez, I see PawCare handles about 800 patients monthly — that's impressive for a solo practice. Which parasiticide are you currently recommending for flea and tick prevention?"

Dr: "We've been using [Competitor Brand X] topical for about 3 years."

Rep: "Got it. And roughly what percentage of your patient base is on a consistent prevention program?"

Dr: "Honestly, probably around 25%. It's hard to get compliance up."

**Problem (5 min)**
Rep: "That 25% compliance is actually common — what do you think is the biggest barrier? Is it price resistance from pet owners, or something else?"

Dr: "It's a mix. Some owners find the topical messy to apply. Others forget monthly. And yes, some push back on cost."

Rep: "When clients find it messy or forget, what typically happens? Do you see them come back with infestations?"

Dr: "Yes, every summer we get a wave of flea infestation cases that could have been prevented. It's frustrating."

Rep: "How does your team feel about having those reactive conversations — 'your pet has fleas because you stopped prevention'?"

Dr: "My tech hates it. It's awkward and the client feels judged."

**Implication (6 min)**
Rep: "If only 25% of your 800 patients are on prevention, that means about 600 pets are unprotected. In flea season, what does the treatment cost look like per infestation case?"

Dr: "Usually $80-150 per case including exam, treatment, and home environment advice."

Rep: "So if even 10% of unprotected pets come in with infestations — that's 60 cases at $100 average — $6,000 in reactive treatments. But your margin on reactive care vs. proactive prevention programs is different, right?"

Dr: "Much lower. Reactive visits are one-off. Prevention is monthly recurring revenue."

Rep: "So the compliance gap isn't just a pet health issue — it's potentially $50,000+ in annual prevention revenue that's not being captured. Does that number feel directionally correct?"

Dr: "Yeah, probably more actually if you count heartworm prevention too."

**Need-Payoff (3 min)**
Rep: "If you could move compliance from 25% to even 40% — an additional 120 pets on monthly prevention — what would that mean for your practice?"

Dr: "That's significant. Even at $30/month average, that's $3,600 extra monthly. $43,000 annually."

Rep: "And if the product itself was easier to administer — like a palatable chew that owners actually enjoy giving — how would that change the compliance conversation?"

Dr: "It removes the biggest objection. If it's easy and the pet likes it, owners stick with it."

**Transition to Presentation**
Rep: "That's exactly what I wanted to show you today. Our [Product] is a monthly flavored chew with 94% palatability in trials. Let me walk you through how clinics similar to yours have moved compliance from 25% to 45% within 6 months..."

## Scenario 2: Multi-Vet Hospital Group

### Context
- Clinic: Regional Animal Hospital, 5 veterinarians
- 3,000 patients/month across companion animals
- Centralized purchasing managed by practice manager
- Currently split across 3 different parasiticide brands
- Budget set quarterly, next review in 6 weeks
- Key concern: supplier consolidation and margins

### Ideal SPIN Conversation

**Situation (2 min)**
Rep: "Sarah, I understand Regional Animal Hospital handles about 3,000 patients monthly across your five vets. Your purchasing is centralized through you — is that correct?"

Sarah (Practice Manager): "Yes, I handle all pharmaceutical procurement and budget allocation."

Rep: "And currently you're working with multiple parasiticide brands — is that by design or did it evolve over time?"

Sarah: "It evolved. Each vet had preferences, so we ended up with three different brands on the shelf."

**Problem (5 min)**
Rep: "Having three brands — does that create any inventory management challenges?"

Sarah: "Definitely. We carry more SKUs than we need. Some products expire before we use them. It's waste."

Rep: "What does the expired product cost look like annually?"

Sarah: "Last year we wrote off about $4,500 in expired parasiticides. Not huge, but it's pure waste."

Rep: "Beyond inventory, does having multiple protocols create any confusion for the support staff when dispensing?"

Sarah: "Our newer techs sometimes grab the wrong dosage size or confuse the brands. We've had to redo dispensing a few times."

**Implication (6 min)**
Rep: "If dispensing errors lead to an underdose or wrong product, what's the clinical risk?"

Sarah: "It hasn't caused a serious incident yet, but it's a liability concern. Our vets are nervous about it."

Rep: "And from a budget perspective — you're managing three supplier relationships, three sets of terms, three reps calling you. What does that fragmentation cost in your time?"

Sarah: "At least half a day per month just managing orders, reconciling invoices, and dealing with backorders from different suppliers. My time is worth more than that."

Rep: "If I calculate that conservatively — half a day monthly at your billing rate, plus $4,500 in waste, plus the risk exposure — we're looking at $15,000-20,000 in annual cost of fragmentation. Does that align with your sense of it?"

Sarah: "Probably, yes. It's a known problem but consolidation always feels like a big project."

**Need-Payoff (4 min)**
Rep: "What if you could consolidate to a single parasiticide partner that covers your full range — endo, ecto, combination products — with one order, one invoice, and one point of contact?"

Sarah: "That would simplify things enormously. My concern is whether one brand can truly replace all three."

Rep: "If the range covers 95% of your protocols, and the pricing delivers better margins than your current blended cost, would consolidation be worth evaluating?"

Sarah: "Absolutely. If the numbers work and the vets are comfortable with the efficacy data, I'd push for it."

Rep: "And with your quarterly budget review in 6 weeks — is that the right window to present a consolidation proposal?"

Sarah: "Perfect timing actually. Bring me a proposal I can take to the partners."

## Scenario 3: Budget Objection Handling

### Context
- Clinic has expressed interest but says "budget is tight this quarter"
- Rep needs to use SPIN to reframe the budget conversation

### Conversation

Rep: "I hear you on budget being tight. Let me ask — the compliance gap we discussed, where only 200 of your 1,000 patients are on prevention — what's that costing you monthly in missed prevention revenue?"

Vet: "I hadn't really calculated it that way."

Rep: "If 800 unprotected patients could be moved even partially — say 100 more pets at $35/month — that's $3,500 in new monthly revenue. The product cost to your clinic would be about $1,800 for those patients. So the NET new revenue is $1,700 per month."

Vet: "The margin is that good?"

Rep: "Yes — and that's conservative. Most clinics find it self-funding within the first month. Would it help if we structured a 90-day trial on your top 50 clients first, so you can validate the economics before committing to full inventory?"

Vet: "A trial I can do. That doesn't require budget approval."

## Scenario 4: Time-Constrained Call (10 minutes only)

### Context
- Vet is between appointments, gives you only 10 minutes
- Must compress SPIN into a shorter format

### Compressed SPIN

**Situation (1 min — max 1 question, you already researched)**
Rep: "Dr. Chen, I know you're short on time so I'll be brief. I see your practice focuses on cats — roughly 500 feline patients monthly. What's your current feline parasiticide protocol?"

**Problem (2 min — go straight to known pain point)**
Rep: "Many cat-focused practices tell me the biggest challenge is owner compliance with topical applications — cats resist and owners give up. Is that true for your practice too?"

**Implication (3 min — one powerful question)**
Rep: "If owners are stopping prevention because application is stressful, and we see flea-allergic dermatitis cases spike every summer — what does that cost you in reactive appointments that could have been preventive revenue?"

**Need-Payoff (2 min)**
Rep: "If there was a palatable oral option specifically formulated for cats with 89% voluntary acceptance, would that change your compliance recommendation?"

**Close (2 min)**
Rep: "Let me leave you a sample box for 10 patients. Try it for 30 days. I'll check back on [date]. If your clients and cats respond well, we can discuss stocking. Fair?"

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

### 1. Feature Dumping (No SPIN)
❌ "Let me tell you about our product. It has broad-spectrum efficacy, 94% palatability, monthly dosing, and costs $X per unit."
✅ Use SPIN to establish need FIRST, then present features as solutions to stated problems.

### 2. Premature Closing
❌ "So can I sign you up for an order today?" (before establishing value)
✅ Complete the SPIN cycle. Need-Payoff should make the close feel natural.

### 3. Ignoring Budget Signals
❌ Pushing for a large initial order when the customer signals budget constraints.
✅ Offer a structured trial, start small, prove ROI, then expand.

### 4. Spending 15 Minutes on Situation Questions
❌ "Tell me everything about your practice..." (wastes time, feels like an interrogation)
✅ Research beforehand. Ask max 2-3 situation questions to confirm what you know.

### 5. Not Getting a Commitment
❌ Ending with "I'll follow up sometime"
✅ Always end with a specific next step: trial date, follow-up call, proposal deadline.
